\chapter*{\Large \center Abstract}

Large Language Models have demonstrated exceptional capabilities in numerous natural language processing tasks, yet they still suffer from the critical problem of ``hallucinations'' i.e., the generation of factually incorrect information presented with high confidence. Although analyzing the internal states of models (using probing techniques) has proven effective for detecting these anomalies, and steering techniques can mitigate them at inference time, most current approaches require training classifiers or steering modules specific to each individual architecture, limiting their scalability and general applicability.


This work explores the feasibility of transferable mechanisms for both hallucination detection and mitigation across different LLM architectures. On the \textbf{detection} side, we investigate whether and how detection capabilities learned on a ``Trainer'' model can be transferred to a different ``Tester'' model, developing and comparing various latent space alignment methodologies, ranging from hybrid approaches to non-linear projections, culminating in the proposed \textit{One-For-All} unified prober. On the \textbf{mitigation} side, we adapt and evaluate two steering techniques --- \textit{TruthX}, based on contrastive disentangled representation learning, and \textit{SLiMTruth}, an adaptation of State-wise Linear Modulation to hallucination reduction --- analyzing their effectiveness in standard, cross-dataset, and cross-model transfer settings.

The results demonstrate that transferring truthfulness-related features for detection is highly effective, especially when using the \textit{One-For-All} approach, and that latent space alignment learned in simpler contexts is surprisingly robust and transferable even to more complex contexts. Regarding mitigation, SLiMTruth achieves the best absolute hallucination rate reductions in standard settings (up to $-89.9\%$), while TruthX exhibits superior cross-model transferability, particularly on complex tasks.
This work highlights the potential of alignment and steering techniques for creating cross-model safety systems for both detecting and mitigating hallucinations, while also outlining the current limitations --- particularly the vulnerability of low-error settings to over-intervention and the challenges of cross-model transfer for layer-specific modulation approaches.
