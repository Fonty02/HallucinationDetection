\section{Goals and Objectives}
The public release of GPT-3 \cite{GPT3} by OpenAI marked a significant turning point in Natural Language Processing (NLP), paving the way for a new era of Large Language Models (LLMs) capable of maintaining conversations as fluently and naturally as a human. The introduction of chatbots marked only the inception of a paradigm shift. Currently, LLMs serve as essential infrastructure across private, academic, and industrial domains, offering a near-infinite range of applications

However, one of the main obstacles to the widespread and safe adoption of these technologies in critical areas is the phenomenon of \textit{hallucinations}. Despite syntactic coherence, LLMs occasionally tend to generate factually incorrect or fabricated information with a high degree of confidence \cite{surveyHallucination}. In contexts such as healthcare, legal support, or education, such hallucinations can lead to serious consequences (e.g., misdiagnoses, incorrect legal advice, or spread of misinformation). It results from the inherently stochastic character of LLMs. Since these models lack counting capabilities and any logical reasoning abilities, the only way to create text is by applying statistics that they acquired when trained on huge bodies of text. The technique is called next-token prediction and works as follows: after receiving particular context or a string of tokens, an LLM will calculate the most likely next token in accordance with its internal distribution, where a token can be simply considered as entire words or pieces thereof. An example of the hallucination that was typical of early LLMs is:
\begin{quote}
    \textbf{User:} How many r's are there in the word strawberries?\\
    \textbf{LLM:} 2
\end{quote}

Currently, techniques to mitigate this problem without making any intervention are often based on external fact verification (\textit{retrieval-augmented generation}) \cite{RAG}, specific \textit{fine-tuning}, or \textit{Chain of Thought} (CoT) \cite{CoT}, but these techniques are not infallible, and it is therefore crucial to understand when a model is hallucinating and when it is not or to manipulate the generation process to reduce the probability of hallucinations. 

\textit{Probing} of internal activations (or \textit{probing classifiers}) represents an innovative solution to the first problem \cite{Probing}. This methodology allows analyzing the internal state of the model during generation, hypothesizing that information about the truthfulness of a statement is encoded in the latent space of neurons, regardless of the final textual output which is generated stochastically as it is influenced by multiple factors (temperature, top-k sampling, etc.). 
Several recent studies have demonstrated that it is possible to train simple linear classifiers to distinguish true statements from false ones by observing the activations of internal layers, and that generally activations related to hallucinations and those related to correct answers tend to be easily separable. \cite{Geometry}

\textit{Steering} of the generation process is a promising approach to mitigate hallucinations. By manipulating the activations of specific neurons or layers during generation, it is possibile to guide the model's output towards desired behaviors \cite{SteeringDef}. Several studies have shown that it is possible to steer the generation process towards more truthful outputs to reduce hallucinations in LLMs. \cite{TruthForest} \cite{TruthX} \cite{ITI}


It has been shown that LLMs converge to a shared statistical model of reality, the so called \textit{Platonic Representation Hypothesis} \cite{Platonic}, which suggests that different LLMs may share a common internal representation of the world, including the phenomenon of hallucinations. Anyway most research has focused on creating probes and steering vectors specific to a single model or a specific architecture, leaving the problem of transferability relatively unexplored.

This work aims to study whether there exists a universal representation of hallucinations in LLMs, regardless of the underlying architecture. In particular, the goal is to develop a general \textit{prober} capable of detecting hallucinations in different models without the need for specific training for each of them and a general \textit{steering} technique to mitigate hallucinations in different models (at most with a preliminary alignment between the latent spaces, as these differ between different models).

The main objectives of this project are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Analyze and compare the internal activations of different LLM families (e.g., Gemma, Llama) to identify common patterns associated with the hallucination phenomenon.
    \item Build a universal \textit{prober} capable of transferring hallucination detection capability from one model to another without additional supervision on the target model.
    \item Develop a general \textit{steering} technique to mitigate hallucinations in different models, with minimal or no need for model-specific adjustments.
\end{itemize}
