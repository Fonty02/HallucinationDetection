\chapter{Background}

This chapter introduces the fundamental concepts necessary to understand the work carried out.
The concepts of attention, LLMs, the hallucination phenomenon, probing,
and steering will be presented, with particular attention to recent advances in mechanistic
interpretability and causal analysis.

\section{Transformer Overview}


The self-attention mechanism was first introduced in 2017 by Vaswani et al.\ ~\cite{AttentionIsAllYouNeed} in the seminal
paper \emph{Attention is All You Need} and represents the
foundation of the Transformer architecture, which has revolutionized the field of NLP and beyond. The crucial difference between Transformers and previous sequential
models—such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and their variants—lies in input processing:
RNNs process data sequentially and maintain a hidden state that compresses previous information,
while Transformers exploit the self-attention mechanism to process the entire input sequence
simultaneously, allowing them to capture relationships between tokens regardless of their
distance in the sequence. With very long inputs, RNNs tend to lose early information, whereas
Transformers are not subject to this limitation.


\subsection{Transformer Architecture}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth, height=0.6\textwidth]{images/Transformer.png}
    \caption{Transformer Architecture.}
    \label{fig:Transformer_Architecture}
\end{figure}

A Transformer is composed of two main blocks: \textit{Encoder} and \textit{Decoder}.

\subsubsection{Encoder}
The encoder consists of a series of identical layers placed in sequence, each containing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multi-Head Self-Attention}: allows the model to focus on different parts of the
          input sequence simultaneously, capturing complex relationships between tokens.
    \item \textbf{Layer Normalization and Residual Connections}: to improve training stability and
          facilitate gradient flow, each sub-layer is followed by normalization and a residual
          connection.
    \item \textbf{Feed-Forward Neural Network (FFNN)}: a fully connected feed-forward network that
          further processes the representations obtained from self-attention.
    \item An additional Layer Normalization and Residual Connections layer.
\end{itemize}
The main role of the encoder is to transform the input sequence into an internal representation
(embedding) that captures semantic relationships between tokens.

\subsubsection{Decoder}
The decoder also consists of a series of identical stacked layers, each containing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Masked Multi-Head Self-Attention}: similar to the encoder mechanism, but with a
          causal mask that prevents the model from ``looking ahead'' during training, ensuring
          autoregressive token generation.
    \item \textbf{Layer Normalisation and Residual Connections}: similarly to the encoder, to
          improve training stability.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Head Cross-Attention on Encoder Output}: allows the decoder to attend to
          relevant parts of the encoder's output representation.
    \item A further Layer Normalisation and Residual Connections layer.
    \item \textbf{Feed-Forward Neural Network (FFNN)}: as in the encoder, to further process
          representations.
    \item An additional Layer Normalisation and Residual Connections layer.
\end{itemize}

It is worth noting that modern LLMs predominantly adopt a \textit{decoder-only} architecture,
which has proven highly effective for generative tasks.


\subsection{Self-Attention Mechanism}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth, height=0.35\textwidth]{images/Attn.png}
    \caption{Self-Attention Mechanism Representation.}
    \label{fig:Self_Attention_Mechanism}
\end{figure}

The self-attention mechanism allows the model to weight the importance of different tokens in the
input when processing a particular one. The computation involves three distinct projection
matrices: Query (\(Q\)), Key (\(K\)), and Value (\(V\))~\cite{AttentionIsAllYouNeed}. The
attention output is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:attention}
    \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
\end{equation}
\equationcaption{Scaled Dot-Product Attention}

where \(d_k\) is the dimension of the keys. The softmax normalises the attention scores so that
the model focuses on the most relevant tokens. The \textit{multi-head attention} variant extends
this concept by performing multiple attention computations in parallel, allowing the model to
capture different types of relationships between tokens from distinct representation subspaces.
See Figure~\ref{fig:Self_Attention_Mechanism} for a visual representation of the self-attention mechanism.




\subsection{Mechanistic Interpretability of Attention Heads}

As models became bigger and harder to understand, researchers started focusing more on figuring out how they work inside.
A major focus of this effort has been the mechanistic interpretability of attention
heads~\cite{CausalHeadGating}. Early research involved statistical analysis of attention
patterns, but the field has since advanced towards \textit{mechanistic interpretability}—the goal
of reverse-engineering the specific algorithms computed by individual neurons and heads.

This line of work has uncovered surprisingly specific and algorithmic functions performed by
individual heads. Some heads appear to function as \textbf{Bloom filters}, dedicated to answering
whether a specific token has appeared before in the sequence~\cite{BloomFilters}. Others play a
role in \textbf{copy suppression}, actively preventing verbatim self-repetition~\cite{CopySuppression}.
Further analysis has uncovered heads that function as \textbf{linear counters}, decoding the
value of numbers with near-perfect accuracy across all layers~\cite{MechanisticCounting}.

The gold standard for establishing the causal role of a model component is through
\textbf{activation patching}~\cite{ActivationPatching}. This technique takes two forward passes
and swaps the activation contribution of a specific component between them. If the patched output
recovers the correct behaviour, it provides strong evidence that the component causally mediates
the difference between the two inputs.



\section{Large Language Models}


LLMs represent the natural evolution of Transformer architectures applied at massive scale. An
LLM is essentially a probabilistic model trained on enormous text corpora (on the order of
trillions of tokens) with the fundamental goal of predicting the next token in a sequence given
the previous context. Formally, given a sequence of tokens \(x = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_t)\), the
model estimates the conditional probability distribution \(P(x_{t+1} \mid x_1, \ldots, x_t)\).

The evolution from previous NLP models to current LLMs (such as the GPT series, Llama, and
Claude) is characterised by the \textit{scaling law} phenomenon~\cite{ScalingLaws}, according to
which model performance improves predictably as the number of parameters, the amount of training
data, and computational power increase. An emergent characteristic of LLMs is \textit{In-Context
Learning}, i.e., the ability to learn new tasks simply by observing examples in the prompt,
without updating model weights.


\subsection{LLM Lifecycle}

The typical lifecycle of an LLM is divided into distinct phases:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pre-training (unsupervised)}: The most computationally expensive phase. The
          model is trained in a self-supervised manner on vast datasets (web crawls, books, code)
          to learn language structure, reasoning, and world knowledge. The objective is to
          minimise the cross-entropy loss between the predicted distribution and the actual next
          token. A common practice is \textit{continued pre-training}, where a base model is
          further trained on domain-specific corpora to specialise its knowledge without altering
          its fundamental generative capabilities.

    \item \textbf{Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)}: The pre-trained base model is further trained on
          a smaller, curated dataset of instruction-response pairs to learn to follow user
          instructions and adopt a conversational format.

    \item \textbf{Alignment}: Techniques such as \textit{Reinforcement Learning from Human
          Feedback} (RLHF) or \textit{Direct Preference Optimization}
          (DPO)~\cite{DPO} are applied to ensure the model is safe and aligned with human
          values. In DPO, the model directly optimises for human preferences without requiring
          a separate reward model, often yielding comparable or better results than RLHF.
          This phase penalises the generation of toxic, biased, or unhelpful responses.
\end{enumerate}

Transparency has become an integral component of the modern LLM lifecycle. Documenting
capabilities, limitations, and intended use is now a standard practice through artefacts such as
\textit{Model Cards}, which provide a standardised format for reporting key information about a
model's performance and societal impact.


\subsection{Modern Architectural Innovations}

The evolution of LLMs has been driven not only by scale, but also by significant architectural
innovations. A pivotal development is the \textbf{Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)} ~\cite{MoE} architecture, which
replaces the dense feed-forward networks (FFNs) in each Transformer layer with a collection of
smaller, specialised ``expert'' networks. During processing, a learned router
network determines which subset of experts should handle each token, resulting in a sparse
activation pattern. This design allows models to possess a massive number of total parameters
while keeping the per-token computational cost low. Leading models such as
DeepSeek-V3 leverage MoE to achieve unprecedented scale and efficiency. This
architectural choice has profound implications for probing and steering, as interventions must
consider not only the FFN weights but also the router mechanism that selects them.

Another important refinement is the attention mechanism itself. While standard Multi-Head
Attention (MHA) has quadratic complexity with respect to sequence length, newer models employ
\textbf{Grouped-Query Attention (GQA)} \cite{GQA}, which reduces this complexity by having multiple
attention heads share the same Key-Value projections. This optimisation makes it
feasible to train and deploy models with very long context windows. These shifts from dense,
full-headed attention to sparse, grouped-query attention represent a clear trend towards
balancing computational efficiency with the capacity to process extensive information streams.




\section{Hallucinations in LLMs}


Despite their extraordinary generative capabilities, LLMs suffer from a critical limitation known
as \textbf{hallucination}. In the context of artificial intelligence, a hallucination occurs when
a model generates output that is syntactically correct and fluent, but factually incorrect,
logically inconsistent, or unfaithful to the provided source material~\cite{surveyHallucination}.
This phenomenon poses a significant barrier to the reliable deployment of LLMs in high-stakes
domains such as legal, medical, and scientific applications.


%\subsection{Causes of Hallucination}

The root causes of hallucinations are multifaceted. A primary cause is the \textit{lossy
compression} of vast and often noisy or contradictory knowledge from training corpora into a
finite parameter space~\cite{surveyHallucination}. Furthermore, the training objective of
predicting the next token prioritises text fluency and coherence over factual accuracy, creating a
tendency for models to generate plausible-sounding continuations when uncertain. This behaviour is
related to \textit{sycophancy}—the inclination to please the user by confirming their biases
rather than providing accurate information~\cite{sycophancyLLM}.


\subsection{Taxonomy of Hallucinations}

Huang et al.~\cite{surveyHallucination} define three primary types of hallucinations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Factual Hallucinations}: the model produces information that is simply false or
          inaccurate with respect to reality.
    \item \textbf{Logical Hallucinations}: the model generates responses that are logically
          inconsistent or contradictory.
    \item \textbf{Context Inconsistencies}: the model contradicts the context explicitly provided
          in the input.
\end{itemize}

A complementary classification~\cite{surveyHallucination2} distinguishes between
\textit{intrinsic} and \textit{extrinsic} hallucinations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intrinsic Hallucinations}: the model generates content that conflicts with the
          provided source context.
    \item \textbf{Extrinsic Hallucinations}: the model generates content that cannot be verified
          by the source context, implying the falsehood originates from the model's internal,
          parametric knowledge.
\end{itemize}

More recently, a \textbf{geometric taxonomy}~\cite{GeometricTaxonomy} has proposed that the term
``hallucination'' conflates distinct phenomena that manifest as different geometric signatures in
the model's embedding space. Such a geometrically-grounded classification may lead to more
targeted mitigation strategies. Specialised taxonomies have also been developed for specific
domains, such as code generation, which identifies unique failure modes such as syntax errors,
incorrect logic, and insecure code patterns.


\subsection{Empirical Evidence and Model Comparison}

Benchmarking studies provide concrete evidence of the prevalence and variability of hallucinations
across models. Comparative evaluations reveal significant differences in performance.
For instance, a study profiling legal hallucinations found that GPT-4 had a rate of 58\%, while
Llama~2's rate was as high as 88\%~\cite{LegalHallucinations}. Conversely, an analysis of
search-augmented models found that the Claude family consistently achieved the lowest
hallucination rates (3.0--3.2\%), whereas GPT models exhibited higher rates. A detailed analysis
of the Claude~3 family noted that its superior performance was partly attributable to a greater
willingness to abstain from answering questions on topics about which the model lacked sufficient
information—a behaviour that naturally reduces factual errors. Even top-tier models such as
Claude-3.7 and GPT-o1, however, revealed concerning patterns of reasoning errors, indicating that
deeper logical flaws can persist even in otherwise strong systems.

These empirical findings demonstrate that mitigating hallucinations is a multifaceted challenge
requiring a holistic approach combining better training data, improved model architectures, and
sophisticated post-hoc analysis and control techniques.



\section{Probing}


\textit{Probing} (or \textit{probing classifiers}) is an analysis technique used in Explainable
AI (XAI) to interpret the internal representations of deep neural networks. The fundamental
premise is that the vast, high-dimensional activation spaces of LLMs—values within hidden states
and outputs of various modules at each layer of a frozen model—are not random noise but contain
rich, structured information about the processed input. This principle was empirically supported
by Marks and Tegmark~\cite{Geometry}, who demonstrated that concepts such as truth and falsehood
emerge through linear structures within the model's latent space. It is therefore possible to use
probing classifiers to query these representations and verify whether a model is generating
truthful or hallucinated content.


\subsection{Linear Probing}

The most common and foundational probing strategy is \textbf{linear probing}, which involves
training a simple supervised classifier—typically a linear regression or a shallow
Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)—on top of frozen activations extracted from a specific layer of the
Transformer~\cite{azaria2023internal}. The procedure follows three steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Activation extraction}: a set of inputs is passed through the frozen model, and
          the activation vectors \(h_l\) generated at a specific layer \(l\) are recorded.
    \item \textbf{Prober training}: a simple supervised classifier is trained to predict a target
          property \(y\) of the input based solely on these activations (e.g., the truthfulness of
          a statement).
    \item \textbf{Evaluation}: the classifier's performance indicates how much information related
          to property \(y\) is linearly decodable from that particular layer.
\end{enumerate}

For example, linear probes have been used at each layer to identify where sycophancy-related
information is encoded, producing a layerwise accuracy curve that reflects the distribution of
this trait across the model's depth~\cite{SLiM}.


\subsection{Limitations of Correlational Probing}

A critical insight from recent research is that \textit{high probing accuracy does not imply
causality or practical utility for intervention}. One study demonstrated this disconnect starkly
by showing that single-position activation interventions could achieve 100\% probing accuracy at
certain positions in a Llama-3.2-3B model, yet failed completely to transfer the learned
behaviour when applied during inference~\cite{SinglePositionFails}. This finding challenges the
naive assumption that a strong signal from a probe directly corresponds to a manipulable feature,
and underscores the distributed nature of information encoding in LLMs—knowledge may be spread
across many neurons and layers, making it impossible to elicit the desired behaviour by modifying
only one location. This realisation has spurred the development of more sophisticated probing
strategies.


\subsection{Advanced Probing Methodologies}

Several advanced probing methods have been proposed to capture richer structural relationships and
provide more robust signals.

\textbf{Claim-Conditioned Probability (CCP)}, proposed by Fadeeva et al.~\cite{CCP}, assesses
the robustness of a statement by comparing its probability to that of counterfactual variants
generated by masking important tokens and regenerating the sequence conditionally. A low CCP score
suggests that the model itself considers the generated statement fragile or highly dependent on
specific, potentially spurious, elements of the input.

\textbf{Prompt-Guided Internal States for Hallucination Detection (PRISM)}, proposed by Zhang et
al.~\cite{PRISM}, utilises specifically designed prompt templates to guide the model's hidden
states, making structural differences between truthful statements and hallucinations more
pronounced and easier for a downstream classifier to distinguish.

\textbf{Information Contribution to Residual Stream (ICR)}, proposed by Zhang et
al.~\cite{ICR}, shifts the focus from static representations to the dynamic evolution of hidden
states across the model's layers. By quantifying how sub-modules such as attention mechanisms and
feed-forward networks update the hidden states at each step, ICR creates a dynamic profile that
allows a lightweight classifier to effectively differentiate the patterns associated with truthful
versus hallucinatory generation.


\subsection{Advances in Mechanistic Interpretability of Large Language Models}

Recent research has uncovered an important emergent capability in large models: \textit{evaluation
awareness}. Studies on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct demonstrated that linear probes can successfully
separate real-world evaluation scenarios from simulated ones, indicating that the model's internal
representations adapt based on whether it perceives the interaction as a genuine
test~\cite{EvaluationAwareness}. This growing metacognitive ability extends to resisting
manipulation: models can be fine-tuned to detect the injection of steering vectors with high
accuracy, suggesting they develop an introspective awareness of being manipulated~\cite{DetectSteering}.

The scope of probing has also expanded dramatically beyond linguistic properties. One notable study
discovered that models from the Llama, Qwen, and Gemma families encode the concept of
``jealousy'' as a structured linear combination of other concepts, demonstrating the potential to
reverse-engineer complex mental states~\cite{JealousyConcepts}. Another line of research
identified a \textbf{Format-Agnostic Reasoning Subspace (FARS)} in multiple models, providing
converging evidence for a shared underlying mechanism for reasoning that is independent of the
presentation format of the problem~\cite{FARS}.

Despite these advances, the challenge of detecting all forms of hallucination remains formidable.
Research has highlighted the failure of broad representation-based detectors to generalise
effectively, pointing towards a need for more targeted and context-aware probing strategies.
Crucially, some internal signals appear to be \textit{causally} linked to the hallucination
phenomenon: one study showed that amplifying certain neurons identified via probing directly
increased the rate of hallucinations, confirming their causal role in generating false
information.



\section{Steering}


\textit{Steering} is a technique used to guide the behaviour of LLMs towards desired outputs or
away from undesired ones by manipulating the model's internal representations at inference time.
Unlike traditional fine-tuning, which permanently alters model weights, steering modifies
activations on a per-prompt basis without any changes to the base model parameters—offering a
lightweight and adaptive form of control~\cite{ITI}. The main idea is to identify specific
directions in the model's latent space that correspond to certain attributes (e.g., truthfulness,
toxicity, emotional tone) and inject a carefully crafted vector to nudge the model's trajectory
accordingly.


\subsection{Core Mechanisms}

The central mechanism of activation steering involves manipulating the \textbf{residual stream},
which carries information forward through the layers of the Transformer. Several intervention
types have been proposed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Additive steering}: a pre-computed steering vector \(\delta^*\) is added
          directly to the activations at one or more layers during the forward pass.
    \item \textbf{Multiplicative/scaling steering}: the magnitude of the activations is adjusted
          rather than shifted.
    \item \textbf{Affine transformations}: both scaling and shifting operations are applied
          simultaneously.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{State-wise Linear Modulation (SLiM)}, proposed by Hiremath et al.~\cite{SLiM}, is an
example of the affine approach: it uses a state vector to learn specific scaling and shifting
parameters applied to the intermediate features (residual stream) of the model, allowing dynamic
control over diverse attributes such as sentiment intensity or continuous toxicity levels.


\subsection{Automated and Causal Steering}

A significant advancement has been the move from manually designed interventions to
\textit{learning} steering vectors from data. \textbf{Contrastive Activation
Steering}~\cite{ContrastiveSteering} learns a steering vector by maximising the likelihood of
target samples (e.g., factual sentences) while minimising that of contrasting non-target samples.
\textbf{Generative Causal Mediation (GCM)}~\cite{GCM} offers a more principled approach by
selecting model components from contrastive long-form generations and using them to construct a
steering intervention.


\subsection{Steering for Hallucination Mitigation}

Several methods specifically apply steering to mitigate hallucinations.

\textbf{TruthX}, proposed by Zhang et al.~\cite{TruthX}, employs an autoencoder to learn a
low-dimensional ``truthful space'' within the model's latent space. During inference, this space
is used to steer activations towards more truthful outputs while preserving the original semantic
content, effectively projecting generation onto a manifold of factually coherent possibilities.

\textbf{Truth Forest}, proposed by Chen et al.~\cite{TruthForest}, enhances truthfulness by
uncovering hidden truth representations using multi-dimensional orthogonal probes. It creates
multiple orthogonal bases for modelling truth and introduces a ``Random Peek'' technique that
samples truth-related features from an extended range of sequence positions. During inference,
these orthogonal direction vectors are weighted and used to adjust the model's internal states,
shifting generation towards more truthful outputs without requiring additional fine-tuning.

\textbf{Inference-Time Intervention (ITI)}, proposed by Li et al.~\cite{ITI}, operates by
shifting model activations during inference to enhance truthfulness. It identifies a sparse set of
attention heads with high linear probing accuracy for truthfulness and shifts the activations
along these truth-correlated directions during generation.

On a more fine-grained level, \textbf{Post-hoc Attention Steering for LLMs (PASTA)}~\cite{PASTA}
intervenes in the attention modules after the self-attention computation to explicitly direct the
model's focus towards user-specified parts of the input, thereby guiding generation to align with
key elements. This level of specificity is crucial because different components of the network are
responsible for different aspects of processing.


\subsection{Limitations: Non-Surjectivity and Safety Risks}

Despite its promise, steering presents important limitations. Research has shown that steered
activations are often \textit{non-surjective}: the set of outputs reachable through steering does
not cover the full spectrum of the model's potential behaviours~\cite{NonSurjective}. This
implies that steering is a constrained form of control and that some desired behaviours may be
inaccessible through simple additive or multiplicative means.

More critically, perhaps the most important challenge is what can be termed the
\textbf{safety paradox}: steering vectors designed to enhance performance can systematically
erode the model's pre-existing safety alignment~\cite{SafetyPitfalls}, making it more susceptible
to generating harmful content it previously avoided. This creates a significant vulnerability,
turning a tool for control into a potential vector for harm.

In response, some LLMs have been observed to develop \textbf{endogenous resistance}: when
subjected to steering interventions, these models may attempt to recover mid-generation to produce
a safer or more aligned output, effectively resisting external manipulation~\cite{EndogenousResistance}.
Furthermore, recent work has shown that models can be fine-tuned to detect the injection of
steering vectors with high accuracy~\cite{DetectSteering}, suggesting the development of
introspective awareness of manipulation. These dynamics transform the interaction between human
and machine from a simple control problem into a complex adversarial game, underscoring the
urgent need for safer steering techniques that do not compromise the fundamental safety guarantees
built into these models.
