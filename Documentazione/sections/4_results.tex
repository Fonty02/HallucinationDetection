\chapter{Results and Discussion}

This chapter presents and discusses the results obtained from the experiments conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of the universal prober in detecting hallucinations in LLMs, together with the effectiveness of the proposed mitigation techniques.

\section{Hallucination Detection}

The in-domain results reveal a clear hierarchy among the transfer approaches. On both BeliefBankFacts and BeliefBankConstraints, the \textit{One-For-All} method is the strongest overall. It reaches very high tester performance in both transfer directions, up to 0.9628 AccTe and 0.9905 AUROC on BeliefBankFacts and up to 0.9947 AccTe and 0.9998 AUROC on BeliefBankConstraints, clearly outperforming the classical alignment methods. This dominance does not extend uniformly to HaluEval. In the Gemma$\rightarrow$Llama direction the best detector is the simple \textit{RidgeRegressor} (up to 0.6430 AccTe), with One-For-All notably weaker (between 0.546 and 0.567 AccTe); in the opposite direction, Llama$\rightarrow$Gemma, One-For-All recovers its lead (between 0.718 and 0.741 AccTe, up to 0.8172 AUROC). At the bottom of the ranking, \textit{CCA} and \textit{ReducedNonLinear} are the least robust, frequently collapsing to near-random performance on HaluEval and under the harder transfer settings.

These numbers point to a marked separation in difficulty between the three datasets. BeliefBankConstraints is the easiest setting for transferred detection, near-perfect with One-For-All in both directions, although the classical approaches are strong only in selected directions (for example Gemma$\rightarrow$Llama with RidgeRegressor) and degrade substantially when the direction is reversed. BeliefBankFacts is still favourable to transfer, but it shows larger method-dependent variability. HaluEval is the hardest dataset by a wide margin: most methods stay between 0.54 and 0.68 AccTe, with only direction-specific exceptions. This is consistent with the PCA projections of the activations (Figures~\ref{fig:gemma-pca-attn} and~\ref{fig:llama-pca-attn}), where the HaluEval points form much noisier and less separable clusters than the BeliefBank ones. The likely cause is the length of the HaluEval prompts, which embed a full dialogue history and an external knowledge passage instead of a single short statement, so the truthfulness signal is diluted over a longer and more heterogeneous context.

A central finding is the \textbf{directional asymmetry} of transfer. The HaluEval behaviour of One-For-All, weak from Gemma to Llama but strong from Llama to Gemma, is the clearest example, and analogous asymmetries appear on BeliefBankConstraints for the linear methods. Transfer quality therefore depends not only on dataset complexity but also on which model supplies the representation and which one receives it. No single component (attention, MLP, or hidden) is consistently best either. In-domain One-For-All transfer is relatively stable across components on BeliefBank, but the component effects become pronounced under harder or shifted conditions, where the same train/test pair can give very different outcomes depending on the chosen component. The choice of component is thus a first-order design decision for universal probing rather than a secondary implementation detail.

\section{Hallucination Mitigation}

SLiMTruth is therefore highly effective when matched to its training domain and highly sensitive when its correction patterns are applied out of domain.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis: TruthX vs.\ SLiMTruth}

Taken together, the two methods occupy complementary niches. In the standard setting, SLiMTruth is superior and consistently effective, giving the largest and most stable reductions. Under dataset shift both methods are vulnerable toward BeliefBankFacts, but the SLiMTruth failures are far larger when the transfer is misaligned. Across models, TruthX is clearly the more viable option, especially on HaluEval, where SLiMTruth tends to collapse.

A recurring thread through all of these comparisons is the \textbf{BeliefBankFacts vulnerability}. Because BeliefBankFacts starts from a very low baseline hallucination rate, interventions trained on noisier domains tend to over-correct and introduce new errors. The resulting transferability hierarchy is intuitive: the standard regime (same model, same dataset) is the strongest, with SLiMTruth best overall; cross-dataset transfer (same model, different dataset) is viable only in some directions and carries a high risk toward BeliefBankFacts; and cross-model transfer (different model, same dataset) is currently reliable only for TruthX, and only in selected scenarios, mainly HaluEval.
