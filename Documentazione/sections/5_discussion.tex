\chapter{Discussion}

This chapter discusses the results obtained from the experiments conducted to evaluate the different approaches proposed for hallucination detection in LLMs. The main objective is to analyze the effectiveness of transferability methods and to understand the underlying factors that influence their performance across different models, datasets, and layer types.

\section{Hallucination Detection}

\subsection{Comparison Between Methods}
The results show that the suggested transferability methods are fairly equivalent to each other, presenting very similar metrics on both the tester and trainer. The study therefore demonstrates that it is possible to transfer probers for hallucination detection between different models, especially for simple factual verification tasks. The \textit{One-For-All} approach proves to be the most promising method for building a "universal prober".

\subsection{Dataset Analysis}

The results highlight a clear distinction between datasets based on simple facts (BeliefBank) and more complex ones (HaluEval).

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BeliefBankFacts and BeliefBankConstraints}: On these datasets, all models and approaches achieve very high performance, with accuracies often exceeding 95\%. This suggests that the distinction between true and false statements about general knowledge facts is encoded very clearly and linearly separable in the activation spaces of LLMs. Transferability is therefore high.
    
    \item \textbf{HaluEval}: This dataset proves to be significantly more difficult. Accuracies drop to around 70-80\% for all approaches. In this scenario, the \textit{FullLinear} often clearly outperforms the transfer approaches. This indicates that the "directions" encoding hallucinations in more complex contexts are much more model-specific and less transferable through the tested alignment techniques.
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Layer Analysis}

Regarding the layer type (\textit{attn}, \textit{mlp}, \textit{hidden}), no absolute winner emerges across all scenarios. However, it is often noted that \textit{attn} (attention output) and \textit{hidden} (hidden states) activations tend to provide slightly more stable performance compared to \textit{mlp}. The \textit{One-For-All} approach nevertheless demonstrates remarkable robustness regardless of the layer type used, managing to extract useful information from all internal components analyzed.


\subsection{Cross-Domain Analysis}
The cross-domain experiments reveal an interesting asymmetry: the encoding learned on "simple" datasets (BeliefBank) transfers effectively to complex contexts (HaluEval), while the reverse is less performant. This suggests that latent structures of truthfulness are more clearly defined and "universal" in basic factual tasks, while in complex scenarios they become more entangled with model-specific representations, making transferability more challenging.


\section{Hallucination Mitigation}

This section discusses the results obtained from the two mitigation approaches, TruthX and SLiMTruth, analyzing their effectiveness across standard, cross-dataset, and cross-model settings.

\subsection{TruthX}

\subsubsection{Standard Setting}
In the standard setting (Table~\ref{tab:truthx-baseline-standard}), TruthX produces mixed results depending on the model--dataset combination. The most notable success is on \textbf{HaluEval}, where both models achieve substantial hallucination rate reductions: $-34.6\%$ for Gemma and $-50.3\%$ for Llama. This suggests that complex hallucinations involving nuanced reasoning are particularly amenable to the contrastive editing mechanism of TruthX.

On \textbf{BeliefBankConstraints}, TruthX achieves modest but consistent reductions ($-6.6\%$ for Gemma, $-6.8\%$ for Llama), indicating a limited but positive effect on constraint-based factual consistency.

However, TruthX exhibits a critical failure on \textbf{BeliefBankFacts}: for Llama, the hallucination rate increases dramatically from $6.72\%$ to $28.12\%$ ($+318.3\%$), and for Gemma it also slightly worsens ($+16.3\%$). This paradoxical behavior can be attributed to the low baseline hallucination rate on BBF: when the model is already highly accurate, the steering intervention disrupts well-calibrated representations rather than correcting them.

\subsubsection{Cross-Dataset Transfer}
The cross-dataset results (Table~\ref{tab:truthx-cross-dataset}) reveal an asymmetric transferability pattern. Steering vectors trained on \textbf{BBC or BBF transfer effectively to HaluEval}, achieving reductions comparable to or even exceeding the standard setting (e.g., BBC$\to$HE on Llama: $-42.4\%$; BBF$\to$HE on Gemma: $-33.7\%$). This is consistent with the hypothesis that simple factual tasks encode a general-purpose ``truthfulness direction'' that is beneficial even in complex evaluation contexts.

Conversely, \textbf{transfer towards BBF is consistently harmful}. Training on BBC and evaluating on BBF increases the hallucination rate by $+142.4\%$ for Llama and $+17.8\%$ for Gemma; training on HE and evaluating on BBF yields $+117.1\%$ for Llama and $+18.5\%$ for Gemma. This confirms the vulnerability of BBF to over-intervention: the steering vectors learned from datasets with higher error rates apply corrections that are too aggressive for a task where the model is already performing near-perfectly.

Transfer towards BBC is moderately effective or neutral ($-6.4\%$ for BBF$\to$BBC on Gemma; $-3.1\%$ for BBF$\to$BBC on Llama), while HE$\to$BBC yields mixed results ($-4.5\%$ Gemma, $+4.8\%$ Llama).

\subsubsection{Cross-Model Transfer}
The cross-model results (Tables~\ref{tab:truthx-cross-model-gemma-to-llama} and~\ref{tab:truthx-cross-model-llama-to-gemma}) use Procrustes alignment to map the representation spaces between Gemma and Llama. Once again, \textbf{HaluEval benefits the most}: Gemma$\to$Llama achieves $-38.0\%$ and Llama$\to$Gemma achieves $-36.7\%$, demonstrating that TruthX steering vectors, when properly aligned, can transfer meaningfully across architectures for complex hallucination mitigation.

On BBC, reductions remain modest ($-3.0\%$ and $-7.6\%$), while on BBF the recurring vulnerability manifests again with a $+98.2\%$ increase for Gemma$\to$Llama, although Llama$\to$Gemma achieves a mild improvement ($-8.7\%$).

Overall, Procrustes alignment proves to be a viable strategy for cross-model TruthX transfer, particularly for HaluEval, but insufficient to prevent the BBF degradation effect.


\subsection{SLiMTruth}

\subsubsection{Standard Setting}
In the standard setting (Table~\ref{tab:slim-baseline-standard}), SLiMTruth demonstrates consistent and often dramatic hallucination rate reductions \textbf{across all model--dataset combinations}. This represents a major advantage over TruthX, which fails in certain configurations.

The most striking result is on \textbf{Llama--BBF}, where the hallucination rate drops from $6.72\%$ to $0.68\%$ ($-89.9\%$), virtually eliminating all hallucinations. On BBC, both models achieve substantial reductions: $-59.1\%$ (Gemma) and $-51.2\%$ (Llama). On HaluEval, the improvements are also noteworthy: $-27.6\%$ (Gemma) and $-57.9\%$ (Llama).

The SLiM module's layer-specific intervention, which operates by modifying a single transformer layer's output, appears to be more targeted and less disruptive than TruthX's global contrastive editing. Crucially, it avoids the BBF degradation observed with TruthX, successfully reducing hallucination rates even when the baseline is already very low.

\subsubsection{Cross-Dataset Transfer}
The cross-dataset results (Table~\ref{tab:slim-cross-dataset}) reveal that SLiMTruth is significantly more fragile than TruthX under domain shift. While some transfer directions remain effective, others produce catastrophic failures.

\textbf{Positive transfer}: Training on \textbf{BBF and evaluating on BBC or HE} yields good results. For Llama, BBF$\to$BBC achieves $-46.0\%$ and BBF$\to$HE achieves $-40.8\%$. For Gemma, BBF$\to$HE achieves $-33.3\%$ and BBF$\to$BBC achieves $-14.2\%$. Similarly, \textbf{BBC$\to$HE} produces consistent reductions ($-32.5\%$ Gemma, $-43.2\%$ Llama), confirming the pattern observed in TruthX where simple-fact steering transfers well to complex tasks.

\textbf{Catastrophic failure}: Transfer \textbf{towards BBF} is disastrous. BBC$\to$BBF causes the hallucination rate to skyrocket: from $2.86\%$ to $32.31\%$ on Gemma ($+1029.7\%$) and from $6.72\%$ to $65.98\%$ on Llama ($+881.8\%$). HE$\to$BBF is also harmful ($+31.5\%$ Gemma, $+252.2\%$ Llama). These failures are far more severe than those observed with TruthX, indicating that SLiM's single-layer intervention, while highly effective in-domain, is extremely sensitive to mismatches between training and evaluation distributions.

This behavior can be explained by the fact that the SLiM module learns layer-specific, dataset-dependent correction patterns. When the training data involves a high hallucination rate (BBC, HE) and the evaluation data has a very low rate (BBF), the module over-corrects, introducing errors where the original model was reliable.

\subsubsection{Cross-Model Transfer}
The cross-model results (Tables~\ref{tab:slim-cross-model-gemma-to-llama} and~\ref{tab:slim-cross-model-llama-to-gemma}) reveal the most critical limitation of SLiMTruth: \textbf{cross-model transfer is almost entirely ineffective or harmful}.

For Gemma$\to$Llama, only BBC shows a marginal reduction ($-5.8\%$), while BBF increases by $+85.3\%$ and HE suffers a catastrophic increase of $+248.6\%$. For Llama$\to$Gemma, \textbf{all three datasets degrade}: BBF $+91.6\%$, BBC $+58.1\%$, HE $+133.0\%$.

This stands in stark contrast to TruthX, which achieves meaningful cross-model improvements on HaluEval ($-38.0\%$ and $-36.7\%$). The failure of SLiMTruth in the cross-model setting can be attributed to the nature of its intervention: the SLiM module modifies the output of a specific transformer layer, and the optimal layer varies greatly between models (e.g., layer 27 for Gemma-BBC vs.\ layer 8 for Llama-BBC). Even with Procrustes alignment of the representation spaces, the transformed activations fail to produce appropriate corrections in the target model's computational flow because the SLiM module is fundamentally tied to the dynamics of a specific layer in the source model architecture.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis: TruthX vs.\ SLiMTruth}

The comparison between the two mitigation approaches reveals complementary strengths and weaknesses:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standard setting}: SLiMTruth is clearly superior, achieving consistent reductions across all configurations, including the BBF setting where TruthX fails. The most notable difference is on Llama-BBF, where SLiMTruth achieves $-89.9\%$ while TruthX produces $+318.3\%$.
    
    \item \textbf{Cross-dataset transfer}: Both approaches share the vulnerability of transferring towards BBF, but SLiMTruth's failures are orders of magnitude worse (up to $+1029.7\%$ vs.\ TruthX's $+142.4\%$). For transfer towards HaluEval, both approaches perform similarly well, confirming that truthfulness representations learned from simple factual tasks generalize to complex hallucination scenarios.
    
    \item \textbf{Cross-model transfer}: TruthX with Procrustes alignment significantly outperforms SLiMTruth, particularly on HaluEval where it achieves $\sim$$-37\%$ reductions compared to SLiMTruth's catastrophic increases ($+133\%$ to $+248\%$). This suggests that TruthX's contrastive decomposition into truthful and hallucinated components produces a more transferable representation than SLiM's layer-specific corrections.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{The BBF Vulnerability}
A recurring pattern across both methods is the tendency to \textbf{increase hallucinations on BeliefBankFacts} in non-standard settings. This can be explained by a fundamental asymmetry: BBF has the lowest baseline hallucination rates ($2.86\%$ Gemma, $6.72\%$ Llama), meaning steering interventions calibrated on noisier datasets apply corrections that are disproportionately large relative to the actual error signal. In essence, the correction overcomes the signal, introducing more errors than it resolves.

\subsubsection{Transferability Hierarchy}
Based on the overall results, the following transferability hierarchy emerges for hallucination mitigation:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Standard (same model, same dataset)}: Both methods are effective, with SLiMTruth achieving the best absolute reductions.
    \item \textbf{Cross-dataset (same model, different dataset)}: Effective when transferring from simple to complex tasks (BBF/BBC$\to$HE), but risky in the reverse direction. TruthX is more robust than SLiMTruth.
    \item \textbf{Cross-model (different model, same dataset)}: Only TruthX achieves meaningful improvements, and only on HaluEval. SLiMTruth is not viable in this setting with current alignment strategies.
\end{enumerate}
