\chapter{Conclusions}

This chapter draws together the main outcomes of the experimental campaign and outlines the directions that they suggest for future work.

\section{Summary of Results}
This work investigated transferable mechanisms for both the \textbf{detection} and the \textbf{mitigation} of hallucinations across the Gemma and Llama models, using the BeliefBankFacts, BeliefBankConstraints, and HaluEval datasets.

On the detection side, the \textit{One-For-All} approach proved to be the strongest in-domain transfer method on BeliefBank, achieving near-perfect tester performance on BeliefBankConstraints (up to 0.9947 AccTe and 0.9998 AUROC) and very high results on BeliefBankFacts (up to 0.9628 AccTe and 0.9905 AUROC). No single method, however, was universally best on HaluEval, where performance was substantially lower and strongly direction-dependent: simpler probes such as the RidgeRegressor remained competitive from Gemma to Llama, while One-For-All regained the advantage in the opposite direction. This directional asymmetry turned out to be a core factor throughout, with transfer quality depending heavily on the assignment of source and target models rather than on the choice of method alone. Cross-domain universality was limited: One-For-All transfer was generally weak toward HaluEval, close to chance on average, and strongest between BeliefBankFacts and BeliefBankConstraints. The choice of component also mattered consistently, with no global winner among the attn, mlp, and hidden representations and with component sensitivity growing under domain shift.

On the mitigation side, SLiMTruth gave the largest and most consistent reductions in the standard setting, where it was trained and evaluated on the same model and dataset, reaching reductions of up to $-89.9\%$. TruthX was less dominant in-domain but markedly more transferable, retaining clear cross-model gains on HaluEval of around $-37\%$ in both directions with Procrustes alignment. Cross-dataset transfer was highly asymmetric for both methods: transfers toward BeliefBankFacts were risky and occasionally catastrophic, especially for SLiMTruth. This BeliefBankFacts vulnerability recurred throughout the analysis and is a direct consequence of the dataset's very low baseline hallucination rate, which leaves little genuine error to remove and makes aggressive steering prone to over-correction.

\section{Future Work}
These findings point to several priorities for future research. The pervasive directional asymmetry argues for \textbf{direction-aware transfer strategies} that model the source-to-target relationship explicitly, instead of assuming that transfer is equally effective in both directions. The frequent mismatch between an acceptable AUROC and a low AccTe suggests that \textbf{post-transfer calibration}, optimizing decision thresholds and score scaling after alignment, could recover much of the accuracy lost when a prober crosses into a new model or domain. The over-correction observed on BeliefBankFacts motivates \textbf{adaptive steering strength}, scaling the intensity of the intervention to the estimated baseline risk so as to avoid damaging settings that are already in a low-error regime. For SLiMTruth in particular, the architecture dependence of its layer-wise interventions calls for \textbf{architecture-aware transfer} that learns cross-model layer correspondences jointly with the modulation parameters, rather than relying on fixed layer mappings. Finally, \textbf{richer evaluation protocols} spanning additional domains and explicit robustness checks will be needed to establish whether the truthfulness features discovered here remain stable beyond the datasets considered in this work.
